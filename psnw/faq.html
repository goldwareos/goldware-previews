Hereditary cancer risk

What is genetic cancer risk?

About 5 to 10% of cancers come from an inherited harmful change in a gene, passed down from either your mother's or your father's side. Hereditary risks are often much higher than the general population's and can affect more than one part of the body. A saliva or blood test can find these changes.

Is hereditary cancer passed down in families?

Yes. Many hereditary cancer syndromes are autosomal dominant: a person who carries one of these harmful gene variants has a 50% chance of passing it to each child. Other blood relatives may carry it too, which is why one person's result can matter for the whole family. Dr. Smith can explain who in your family should consider testing.

Who should consider testing?

Testing is worth discussing if you or a close relative has had:

  • Multiple stomach or colon polyps
  • Ovarian cancer
  • Pancreatic cancer
  • Male breast cancer
  • Bile duct or intestinal cancer
  • Multiple melanomas
  • Breast, colon or uterine cancer at an early age, or more than one
  • Aggressive or multiple prostate cancers
  • Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry
  • Other cancer types or combinations

A relative who has already tested positive is another reason to talk with Dr. Smith.

Genes and syndromes

What are BRCA1 and BRCA2?

BRCA1 and BRCA2 are the best-known genes linked to inherited breast and ovarian cancer risk. Harmful variants in them are also linked to pancreatic and prostate cancer. If testing finds a harmful BRCA1 or BRCA2 variant, screening and prevention plans can lower risk or catch cancer earlier.

What is PALB2?

PALB2 is a gene that works closely with BRCA2. An inherited harmful PALB2 variant raises breast cancer risk as well as ovarian, pancreatic, and likely prostate cancer. It is included in the multi-gene tests Dr. Smith uses.

What is Lynch syndrome?

Lynch syndrome is an inherited condition, caused by a harmful variant in genes such as MLH1, MSH2 or MSH6, that raises the risk of colon and uterine cancer and several others, often at younger ages. Knowing about it means colon and other screening can start earlier.

What if my result is negative or uncertain?

A negative result means no harmful variant was found in the genes tested. It does not always mean your risk is average: your personal and family history still guide your care. A variant of uncertain significance (VUS) is a gene change without enough evidence yet to say whether it raises cancer risk; it is not treated as a harmful result, and it may be reclassified as more is learned.

What is hereditary polyposis?

Many polyps in the colon or stomach can be a sign of possible hereditary risk for stomach or colon cancer as well as other cancers. Familial adenomatous polyposis is an example.

Breast cancer risk factors

What are the risk factors for breast cancer?

Risk factors Dr. Smith considers include mammographically dense breasts, atypical ductal or lobular hyperplasia, a strong family history of breast or ovarian cancer and genetic risk, along with factors you can change such as alcohol, smoking, weight, exercise and diet. He looks at all of them together to estimate your overall risk.

I was told I have dense breasts. What does that mean?

Mammography facilities now report breast density for every woman. Density is rated A, B, C or D; C and D count as dense, and about half of women are in the dense range. Dense tissue makes it easier for routine mammograms to miss a cancer, and it is an independent risk factor on its own. Supplemental imaging, such as breast MRI, contrast enhanced mammography or automated whole breast ultrasound, may be worth considering.

My biopsy showed ADH or ALH (atypical hyperplasia). What now?

Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) and atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH) are usually found on core needle biopsies. They mark a higher risk for breast cancer, about 3 to 5 times that of an average-risk woman. There are good options for managing this risk, and Dr. Smith helps build a shared plan based on current national guidelines.

Your visit, insurance and referrals

What happens at my first visit?

Dr. Smith reviews your personal and family cancer history, especially the types of cancer and the ages at first diagnosis, and draws a family tree (pedigree). Together you decide whether genetic testing makes sense. Visits last about 45 minutes, in the office or by telehealth.

What should I bring?

Your family's cancer history: who had cancer, what type and how old they were when it was found. Before the visit, please upload your driver's license, insurance cards and requested forms in the patient portal.

Do you offer telehealth?

Yes. Dr. Smith offers telehealth visits for patients in Idaho and Washington.

What insurance do you take?

Dr. Smith is currently in network for Medicare, Idaho Medicaid, Blue Cross of Idaho, Out-of-state Blue Cross Blue Shield, Regence, Aetna, SelectHealth and PacificSource. Call 208-400-5131 with questions.

Is genetic testing covered, and what does it cost?

Your visits with Dr. Smith are billed to your health insurance. The genetic test itself is processed by an outside laboratory, separately from your visits. Genetic testing is often covered by insurance when national testing guidelines are met, and what you pay depends on your plan. The lab does an immediate cost estimation and, if high, will notify you with billing options.

How long do results take?

If you decide to test, results are typically back in 10 to 21 days. Dr. Smith then goes over them with you and sets up a care plan.

Do I need a referral?

Call 208-400-5131 and the office will let you know. Doctors referring a patient can find what to send under For referring providers.

Can a positive result affect my insurance?

A federal law, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA), stops health insurers and employers with 15 or more employees from using your genetic test results against you. GINA does not cover life, disability or long-term care insurance, so some people review those policies before they test.
