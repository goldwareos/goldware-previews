About 5 to 10% of cancers are inherited. Finding that risk early changes what you and your family can do about it.

Inherited risk can be passed to children or shared with brothers and sisters 50% of the time. It is often much higher than the general population's risk, and it can affect several parts of the body.

The good news: when a genetic predisposition is found, there are active steps to reduce the risk or find cancer earlier. Dr. Smith guides you through the whole process, from the first conversation to long-term follow-up for you and your family.

All questions
Who should consider testing?

Testing is worth discussing if you or a close relative has had:

  • Multiple stomach or colon polyps
  • Ovarian cancer
  • Pancreatic cancer
  • Male breast cancer
  • Bile duct or intestinal cancer
  • Multiple melanomas
  • Breast, colon or uterine cancer at an early age, or more than one
  • Aggressive or multiple prostate cancers
  • Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry
  • Other cancer types or combinations

A relative who has already tested positive is another reason to talk with Dr. Smith.

What are BRCA1 and BRCA2?

BRCA1 and BRCA2 are the best-known genes linked to inherited breast and ovarian cancer risk. Changes in them are also linked to pancreatic and prostate cancer. If testing finds a BRCA change, screening and prevention plans can lower risk or catch cancer earlier. Draft for Dr. Smith

What is Lynch syndrome?

Lynch syndrome is an inherited condition, involving genes such as MLH1, MSH2 and MSH6, that raises the risk of colon and uterine cancer and several others, often at younger ages. Knowing about it means colon and other screening can start earlier. Draft for Dr. Smith

Is genetic testing covered, and what does it cost?

Genetic testing is generally covered by insurance when a person meets specific criteria, and most insurers cover it with little to no out-of-pocket cost.
