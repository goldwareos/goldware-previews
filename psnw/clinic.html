Hereditary Cancer Risk Clinic

The clinic identifies and manages men and women who may have a much higher chance of certain cancers because of an inherited gene change, then supports them and their families with counseling, testing and follow-up.

What the clinic does

Counseling, testing and a plan

About 5 to 10% of cancers are directly related to an inherited harmful gene change from either side of the family. These include breast, ovarian, uterine, colon, pancreatic, skin (including melanoma) and prostate cancers, among others. Finding that change lets you take active steps to reduce risk or find cancer earlier.

  • Pre-test counselingYour options and the reasons for testing, before you decide
  • Genetic testingOffered to patients who wish to test, with a saliva or blood sample
  • Post-test counselingYour results explained and a care plan developed
  • Genetic counselingWith a certified genetic counselor, included in the cost of your testing
  • Family follow-upOngoing care for patients and families with hereditary risk
  • Coordinated careWorking with local and regional specialists

Who should test?

A personal or family history of:

  • Multiple stomach or colon polyps
  • Ovarian cancer
  • Pancreatic cancer
  • Male breast cancer
  • Bile duct or intestinal cancer
  • Multiple melanomas
  • Breast, colon or uterine cancer at an early age, or more than one
  • Aggressive or multiple prostate cancers
  • Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry
  • Other cancer types or combinations

Not sure whether this applies to you? Fill in the risk assessment (PDF) or call 208-400-5131.

Your visit

What happens at your first visit

Dr. Smith reviews your personal and family cancer history, especially the types of cancer in the family and the ages at first diagnosis, so please gather these details beforehand. He draws a family tree (pedigree) to see whether you meet the criteria for genetic testing, if you are interested. Hereditary cancer families tend to show common patterns, often with cancers at much younger ages.

Appointments typically last 45 minutes, in the office or by telehealth. Before your visit, please upload your driver's license, insurance cards and requested forms in the patient portal.

Genetic testing

The test is processed by an outside laboratory, separately from your visits. It is often covered by insurance when national testing guidelines are met, and what you pay depends on your plan. The lab does an immediate cost estimation and, if high, will notify you with billing options. Results are typically back in 10 to 21 days. Ideally the family member who had cancer is tested first; if they are unavailable or prefer not to test, blood relatives can be tested instead.

Genes and syndromes

What testing looks for

BRCA1 and BRCA2

The best-known genes linked to inherited breast and ovarian cancer risk; harmful variants are also linked to pancreatic and prostate cancer.

PALB2

A gene that works closely with BRCA2; an inherited harmful variant raises breast cancer risk as well as ovarian, pancreatic, and likely prostate cancer.

Lynch syndrome

Harmful variants in genes such as MLH1, MSH2 and MSH6 that raise the risk of colon, uterine and other cancers.

Hereditary polyposis

Many polyps in the colon or stomach can be a sign of possible hereditary risk for stomach or colon cancer as well as other cancers. Familial adenomatous polyposis is an example.

CDH1 and other genes

Including genes linked to stomach and breast cancer, Cowden syndrome and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Multi-gene panels

One saliva or blood test can check many genes at once, matched to your family history.

After your results

Ongoing high-risk care

If a harmful variant is found, Dr. Smith puts screening and prevention plans in place to help prevent cancer or find it early, and arranges genetic counseling with a certified genetic counselor, which is included in the cost of your testing. For an autosomal-dominant harmful variant, a carrier has a 50% chance of passing it to each child, and other blood relatives may carry it too, so he also helps your family understand whether they should test.

A negative result means no harmful variant was found in the genes tested. It does not always mean your risk is average: your personal and family history still guide your care. A variant of uncertain significance (VUS) is a gene change without enough evidence yet to say whether it raises cancer risk; it is not treated as a harmful result, and it may be reclassified as more is learned.

Other risk factors, such as dense breasts or atypical hyperplasia on a biopsy, are considered too, so your plan reflects your whole picture. A federal law (GINA) protects you from genetic discrimination by health insurers and larger employers, but not by life, disability or long-term care insurers.
